## A Note from 2026

I wrote this post in October 2024, shortly after finishing my M.S., as a hands-on way to learn PCA and Sparse PCA on real market data. Re-reading it two years later, the data pipeline and the plots hold up, but much of the original interpretation does not. I read macro and geopolitical stories into six weeks of 2-minute returns, and some of the methods had problems I did not catch at the time.

In October 2026 I revised the text. The code and data are unchanged, and so is every figure except the rolling-window dashboard, where I fixed a labeling bug (see below). The edits are to the writing, plus a few quick checks against the outputs saved in the [project repository](https://github.com/parvanovkp/RegimeShift); nothing was re-run. The original title was "Detecting Regime Shifts in SP500 Stocks Using PCA and Sparse PCA". I changed it because the project explores structure; it does not detect regime shifts.

**What changed in this revision:**

- I removed the macro and geopolitical narratives (sanctions, BRICS, defense spending, "market complacency"). Nothing in this analysis can test them.
- I corrected readings that depended on the arbitrary sign of principal components. These include the negative correlations with the volatility ETN (VXX), which reflect the ordinary negative relationship between stock returns and volatility. They say nothing about how much risk the market was pricing.
- I corrected factual details:
  - the sample length and dates;
  - the number of components needed for 90% of the variance;
  - the description of the ETF set;
  - which windows had the lowest similarity to the full period;
  - the size of the largest lagged correlations;
  - a few percentages in the sector tables and intraday plots.
- I fixed a labeling bug in the rolling-window dashboard. Once the slider moved, the sector names no longer matched the rows. My original reading of that figure was based on the wrong labels, so I re-read the observations in that section from the underlying data.
- I added caveats where the method limits what can be concluded. I also removed the "Implications" sections on trading, liquidity provision and rebalancing, which the analysis does not support.

**What I would do differently today:**

1. **Remove the overnight gap.** The first 2-minute return of each day spans the overnight move from the previous close. Those 28 returns account for roughly a quarter of the total variance in the standardized data. They change the full-period results only modestly, but they dominate the 9:30-10:00 bucket in the intraday analysis.
2. **Compare against a noise baseline.** With 457 stocks and about 5,500 observations, random matrix theory (the Marchenko-Pastur distribution) gives the range of eigenvalues that pure noise would produce. A back-of-the-envelope check suggests only about 30 components clear the upper edge of that range. Most of the 255 components needed for 90% of the variance are therefore indistinguishable from noise.
3. **Tune the sparsity penalty.** I used sklearn's default, and the resulting "sparse" components still load on most stocks.
4. **Align components before comparing them across fits.** The sign and order of components are arbitrary in every fit. Windows should be matched first, by sign or by comparing subspaces through principal angles, before measuring any change in structure.
5. **Test before calling something a regime shift.** A bootstrap or permutation null for the stability metric is needed to say whether a dip is unusual. Without one, the rolling-window results are descriptive.
6. **Use enough data per fit.** Each intraday bucket has fewer observations (420) than stocks (457), so its sample covariance matrix is singular.

## Introduction

This project applies Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Sparse PCA to high-frequency returns of S&P 500 stocks. The goal was exploratory. I wanted to see:

- what structure these techniques pick up in 2-minute data;
- how that structure lines up with sectors and sector ETFs;
- whether it visibly changes over a few weeks that included the Federal Reserve's first rate cut of the cycle (September 18, 2024).

PCA is a standard tool for this kind of data. It compresses hundreds of correlated return series into a handful of components, the first of which usually tracks the market as a whole. Sparse PCA adds a penalty that pushes many loadings to zero, which can make components easier to read.

The raw download covers all 503 S&P 500 constituents and 20 ETFs at 2-minute intervals from the Yahoo Finance API. After cleaning, the sample is 457 stocks over 28 full trading days, from August 12 to September 19, 2024.

The project was also a way for me to practice end-to-end machine learning work on real data: acquisition, cleaning, modeling and interactive visualization. It is best read as an exploration, not as a set of trading conclusions.

## Data Preparation

### Data Verification and Consistency

The download included all 503 S&P 500 stocks and 20 ETFs at 2-minute intervals. Yahoo Finance only provides 2-minute bars for roughly the last 60 days, which limited the sample length.

I kept regular market hours only, from 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET. This gives 195 bars per trading day and avoids the thin, irregular trading of the pre-market and after-hours sessions.

### Data Cleaning and Preprocessing

The data cleaning process involved the following steps:

1. **Handling Missing Data**: I filled short gaps forward and then backward, up to 5 intervals, and dropped any rows that were still incomplete. In hindsight this has two side effects. Backward filling uses later prices to fill earlier gaps, and every filled price creates an artificial zero return. With a 5-interval limit the effect is probably small, but today I would mask these points instead.

2. **Asset Filtering**: I removed any asset with more than 20% missing data. This kept 457 of the 503 stocks.

3. **Timestamp Synchronization**: All series were aligned to a common time index, so that price movements are compared at the same moments across the dataset.

4. **Return Calculation**: I worked with logarithmic returns rather than prices. Log returns are additive over time and closely approximate percentage changes over short intervals. Because the returns were computed on the stitched-together series, the first return of each day spans the overnight gap (see the note at the top).

5. **Standardization**: I standardized each stock's return series to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. This keeps stocks with different volatilities on a comparable scale.

The cleaned dataset has 5,463 synchronized timestamps, about 97% of the 5,655 in the raw download. These give 5,462 2-minute returns for 457 stocks and 20 ETFs. A handful of stocks have no data on the first day of the download (August 9), so dropping incomplete rows removes almost all of that day. The analyzed sample therefore effectively runs from August 12 to September 19.

## Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA transforms a dataset into a new coordinate system whose axes, the principal components, are ordered by how much variance they explain. This section covers three steps:

- standard PCA on the full sample;
- Sparse PCA;
- a sector-level summary of the Sparse PCA loadings.

### Full Period PCA

I applied PCA to the full sample of 457 stocks. PCA finds a set of orthogonal directions (the principal components) that maximize the variance of the projected data.

For a data matrix $X$, PCA solves the eigenvalue problem:

$$(X^TX)v_i = \lambda_i v_i$$

where $v_i$ are the eigenvectors (principal components) and $\lambda_i$ are the corresponding eigenvalues, which measure the variance explained by each component.

Components are ordered by decreasing explained variance, so PC1 explains the most, PC2 the second most, and so on.

I used sklearn's PCA implementation and kept enough components to explain 90% of the variance.

#### Interpretation of Full Period PCA Results

The cumulative explained variance plot shows how quickly variance is captured. The first component alone explains about 24.5% of the variance and the second about 7.4%. After that the curve flattens: 154 components are needed for 80% of the variance, and 255 (slightly more than half of the 457) for 90%.

That long tail is expected at this frequency. Over 2-minute intervals, much of each stock's return is idiosyncratic noise and market microstructure (bid-ask bounce, asynchronous trading). Correlations between stocks are also weaker than at daily frequency, a pattern known as the Epps effect. So one large market component followed by a long, flat tail is the typical picture, and many of the trailing components are likely indistinguishable from noise (see the note at the top).

PC1 has a positive loading on every stock and behaves like a market factor. Beyond the first few components, principal components usually mix several influences. They should not be read as clean economic factors without further work.

### Sparse PCA

Standard PCA components involve every input variable, which makes them hard to interpret. Sparse PCA adds a penalty that pushes many loadings to exactly zero, so that each component is described by fewer stocks.

In sklearn's formulation, Sparse PCA solves:

$$\min_{U,V} \frac{1}{2}\lVert X - UV \rVert_F^2 + \alpha \lVert V \rVert_{1} \quad \text{subject to } \lVert U_k \rVert_2 \le 1 \text{ for all } k$$

Here the rows of $V$ are the components, that is, the loadings over stocks, which are the part being made sparse. $U$ holds the corresponding component scores over time, and $\alpha$ controls the strength of the sparsity penalty.

I used sklearn's SparsePCA with 10 components and the default penalty, $\alpha = 1$. Two things about the result matter for everything that follows:

- **The components are only mildly sparse.** With the default penalty, each component still has non-zero loadings on roughly 340 to 400 of the 457 stocks. The components end up tilted toward particular sectors rather than isolating a small group of stocks. I should have tuned $\alpha$.
- **The components are not ordered by explained variance.** Unlike standard PCA, sklearn's SparsePCA does not sort them, so the labels PC1 to PC10 are just labels. For example, Sparse PC9 is not the ninth most important component.

In principle, Sparse PCA trades some explained variance for interpretability. The main practical drawbacks are:

- the result depends on the choice of $\alpha$;
- it is more expensive to fit than standard PCA;
- its components are not orthogonal or ranked the way standard principal components are.

## Correlation with ETF Factors

To connect the components to something observable, I correlated each component's scores with the returns of 20 ETFs:

- broad-market funds: SPY, QQQ, DIA and IWM;
- the SPDR sector funds: XLK, XLV, XLF, XLE, XLI, XLP, XLY, XLU, XLB, XLRE and XLC;
- a short-term VIX futures ETN: VXX;
- gold, silver, oil and long-term Treasury funds: GLD, SLV, USO and TLT.

### Methodology

1. **Component Scores**: I projected the standardized stock returns onto the PCA and Sparse PCA loadings to get a time series of scores for each component.

2. **Correlation Analysis**: I computed Pearson correlations between each component's scores and each ETF's standardized returns.

3. **Visualization**: The correlations are shown as heatmaps.

4. **Lag Analysis**: To look for leading or lagging relationships, I repeated the correlations with the ETF returns shifted by up to 5 intervals (10 minutes) in either direction.

#### Lag Analysis

Lead-lag relationships were weak at shifts of up to 10 minutes:

- More than 90% of the lagged correlations are below 0.05 in absolute value.
- The largest are about 0.10 to 0.13, compared with contemporaneous correlations above 0.9.
- All of the largest occur at a one-interval shift with the ETF moving first.

This is roughly what you would expect. Arbitrage keeps liquid ETFs in line with their constituents, so most new information shows up in both within the same 2-minute bar. The small one-bar ETF lead is consistent with some individual stocks' 2-minute prices being slightly stale relative to the more heavily traded ETFs. The lag analysis plots are available [here](https://github.com/parvanovkp/RegimeShift/tree/main/interpretation_results).

So far everything has been static, treating the whole sample as one period. The sample includes the Federal Reserve's 50 basis point rate cut on September 18, 2024, so the natural next question was whether the structure visibly changes over time.

## Rolling Window PCA

To look at how the structure evolves, I repeated the analysis on rolling windows.

### Methodology

I applied both standard PCA and Sparse PCA using rolling windows with the following parameters:

1. **Window Size**: 10 trading days (1,950 2-minute returns)
2. **Step Size**: 1 trading day (195 returns)
3. **Number of Components**: 10

This gives 19 windows. Each is labeled by its last day, from August 23 to September 19. Consecutive windows share nine of their ten days, so they are expected to look similar.

For each window, I:

1. extracted the data for the window;
2. fitted standard PCA and Sparse PCA;
3. computed sector loadings for each of the first 10 components;
4. compared the result with the previous window and with the full sample.

### Stability and Similarity Metrics

Both metrics are computed from the standard PCA results. For each of the first 10 components, I took the sector shares of its positive loadings (the same construction as in the intraday section below) and flattened them into a single vector.

1. **Market Stability** compares consecutive windows using cosine similarity:

   $$\mathrm{Stability}_{i, i+1} = 1 - \mathrm{cosine\_distance}(\mathrm{loadings}_i, \mathrm{loadings}_{i+1})$$

   where $\mathrm{loadings}$ are the flattened sector-share vectors. Higher values mean consecutive windows look more alike.

2. **Similarity to the Full Period** compares each window with the full-sample result:

   $$\mathrm{SimFull}_i = 1 - \mathrm{cosine\_distance}(\mathrm{loadings}_i, \mathrm{loadings}_{\mathrm{full}})$$

   In the dashboard this panel is labeled "Deviation from Full Period". Despite that name, it is a similarity score: lower values mean the window looks less like the full sample.

### Results and Interpretation

#### Sector Loadings Over Time

Stepping through the windows shows a few patterns. In the original version of the dashboard, the sector labels stopped matching the rows once the slider moved, and my original observations were read off those wrong labels. The figure is now fixed, and the observations below were re-read from the underlying data:

1. **Consumer early on**: In every window ending August 23 to September 4, consumer stocks lead half or more of the ten standard-PCA components. Afterwards they lead three or fewer. Consumer is also the largest of my merged sectors, so it tends to lead whenever loadings are spread evenly. This is partly a sector-size effect.

2. **Technology and utilities in PC2**: Utilities lead standard PC2 in the first five windows (ending August 23 to 29) and again in the last one. Technology leads it in every window from August 30 to September 18 except September 3, at 44-54%. In Sparse PCA, PC2 mostly alternates between the same two sectors.

3. **Energy in PC3**: Energy leads PC3 in both methods from late August to mid-September, at 32-49%. In standard PCA that is the windows ending August 30 to September 12; in Sparse PCA, August 29 to September 16.

4. **Financial Services in PC1**: Financial services leads Sparse PC1 in every window ending August 30 to September 16. It also leads standard PC1 in the windows ending September 11 to 17, but only with 17-18%. Standard PC1 is the market factor, so its loadings are spread across all sectors.

5. **Real Estate and Healthcare later**: Real estate leads PC4 in both methods in the windows ending September 13 to 19. From early September, healthcare leads one or more of the later components in most windows.

These observations are descriptive. The sign and order of components can change from one window to the next. Some of these shifts may therefore reflect a component flipping sign or swapping places with another, rather than a real change in market structure.

#### Market Stability

The stability values range from 0.76 to 0.97, and most are above 0.8.

- **Lowest values:** the windows ending August 30 (0.771), September 12 (0.771) and September 19 (0.763).
- **Highest values:** September 6 and September 16 (both 0.972).

In the original version I matched each dip to a news event, such as a producer-price inflation release on September 12 and the Fed's decision on September 18. I no longer think the metric supports that, for two reasons:

1. **Sign flips.** The metric counts only positive loadings. If a component flips sign between windows, the metric counts the other half of its stocks, and this registers as a change in structure. Every component except PC1 flips sign at least twice over the 18 steps.
2. **A high floor.** Converting loadings into sector shares pulls everything toward the same baseline. Even completely unstructured loadings would produce shares that roughly mirror sector sizes, and two windows of random loadings would look nearly identical. A high value therefore does not mean stable structure. It can also mean there is no sector concentration at all.

A quick check against the saved loadings makes both points concrete:

- Aligning each component's sign with the previous window removes the September 12 dip entirely: 0.77 becomes 0.92.
- Replacing the loadings with random numbers gives values around 0.97, the same level as the "peak stability" readings.

After sign alignment, the lowest values are at the steps into the windows ending September 18 and 19, which add the day of the Fed's 50 basis point cut ([WSJ, Sept. 18, 2024](https://www.wsj.com/livecoverage/fed-interest-rate-cut-inflation-live-09-18-2024)) and the day after. August 30 is nearly as low, though. Without a null distribution, I would not call any of this a regime shift.

#### Similarity to the Full Period

Similarity to the full sample generally declines over the period. It ranges from about 0.69 to 0.85 in the windows ending before September 13, and from 0.54 to 0.59 in the windows ending September 13 to 19. The window most similar to the full sample is the one ending September 3 (0.849).

One possible explanation is that the last week of the sample, which contains the Fed decision, differs from the rest. However, the same caveats about sign flips and the missing baseline apply, so I treat this as a descriptive observation rather than a finding.

### Takeaways

The rolling-window analysis is a useful way to visualize how loadings move around over a short sample. In its current form it is not a regime-shift detector. Making it one would need component alignment across windows and a null distribution for the metrics. The result also depends on the window length, and with heavily overlapping windows, consecutive values are not independent.

## Intraday Pattern Analysis

Next I looked at whether sector structure differs by time of day. Intraday patterns such as heavier trading at the open and close are well documented, so it seemed worth checking whether they show up in the components.

### Methodology

1. **Data Segmentation**: I divided the trading day into 13 periods of 30 minutes each:

   $$p_i = [t_i, t_{i+1}), \quad i = 1, 2, ..., 13$$

   where $t_1$ is the market open (9:30 AM) and $t_{14}$ the market close (4:00 PM).

2. **Sparse PCA Application**: For each period $p_i$, I pooled the returns from that half hour across all days and fitted Sparse PCA with 10 components:

   $$X_{p_i} \approx U_{p_i} V_{p_i}$$

   where $X_{p_i}$ is the data matrix for period $p_i$, the rows of $V_{p_i}$ are the sparse loadings, and $U_{p_i}$ holds the component scores.

3. **Sector Loading Calculation**: For each component $j$ and sector $S$ in period $p_i$, the sector loading $SL_{S,j,p_i}$ is:

   $$SL_{S,j,p_i} = \sum_{k \in S} \max(L_{k,j,p_i}, 0)$$

   where $L_{k,j,p_i}$ is the loading of stock $k$ in component $j$ for period $p_i$. These were then normalized across sectors:

   $$NSL_{S,j,p_i} = \frac{SL_{S,j,p_i}}{\sum_{S'} SL_{S',j,p_i}}$$

4. **Visualization**: I produced two charts:

   - an intraday heatmap showing $NSL_{S,j,p_i}$ for all sectors, components and periods;
   - a sector influence plot showing each sector's average influence:

     $$ASI_{S,p_i} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{10} NSL_{S,j,p_i}}{\sum_{S'} \sum_{j=1}^{10} NSL_{S',j,p_i}}$$

**Caveats (added in 2026).** Four problems limit what this section can show:

- **Too few observations per fit.** Each 30-minute bucket pools 15 returns per day over 28 days, so each Sparse PCA fit sees 420 observations of 457 stocks. With fewer observations than variables, the sample covariance matrix is singular and the fitted components are very noisy.
- **The overnight gap.** The 9:30-10:00 bucket contains the 28 overnight-gap returns. These carry far more variance than ordinary 2-minute returns and dominate that bucket.
- **Arbitrary signs.** As in the rolling-window section, only positive loadings are counted, so the results depend on arbitrary component signs.
- **Unrelated component numbers.** Each bucket is a separate fit, so "PC3" at 10:00 and "PC3" at 14:00 need not be the same factor.

I would treat the figures below as a visualization of noisy fits rather than evidence of intraday patterns.

### Limitations and Future Work

The main limitations of this section:

1. **Sample Size per Fit**: Each period's fit has fewer observations than stocks, which is the most serious limitation and is described above.

2. **Overnight Gap**: The opening period mixes overnight moves with ordinary 2-minute returns.

3. **Time Period Specificity**: The sample covers six weeks in August and September 2024 and may not generalize to other periods.

4. **Aggregation Effects**: Pooling returns across days hides day-specific patterns, including responses to scheduled announcements.

5. **Sector Classification**: The merged sector categories, especially the combined consumer category, are coarse.

A more convincing version of this analysis would:

- use a much longer sample, so that each period's fit has many more observations than stocks;
- drop the overnight return, or treat the open separately;
- compare each period with a baseline in which period labels are randomly shuffled, to see whether the differences exceed what noise produces;
- check whether any pattern is stable across separate months.

## Conclusion

What I think holds up:

1. **A single market factor dominates.** PC1 explains about 24.5% of the variance, loads positively on every stock, and correlates at 0.85 or more with broad-market and cyclical-sector ETFs.
2. **Beyond PC1, the variance spectrum is long and flat.** It takes 154 components for 80% of the variance and 255 for 90%. That is expected at 2-minute frequency, where much of the variation is noise.
3. **Several components line up with individual sectors** (utilities, energy, real estate, financials, technology), confirmed by their correlations with the matching sector ETFs. With the default penalty, though, the "sparse" components remain mostly market baskets tilted toward a sector.

What does not hold up:

- **The rolling-window and intraday results are descriptive only.** The stability metric is sensitive to component sign flips and has a high floor, and the intraday fits have fewer observations than stocks. Neither supports claims about regime shifts or tradable intraday patterns.

The most useful thing this project taught me was how easy it is to over-read PCA on market data. If I return to it, the list in the note at the top is where I would start.
